Why Guardrails, Not Just Better Prompts

The failure that motivates every guardrail in this series, and the one-sentence principle that decides whether a fix belongs in your prompt or in your code. Almost no new code on this page; a new way of reading model output instead.

Builds on: the RAG agent. Produces: the grounding-hardened system prompt (checkpoint chapter 5).

The failure: silence becomes a stated negative

The problem

Ask "how many PTO days do new employees get, and does it change over time?" The first half goes beautifully: real retrieval, real accrual policy, real page source. Then, because the retrieved chunk says nothing about rates changing with tenure, the model writes: "No, the accrual rate does not change over time." The document was silent. The model reported the silence as a "no."

Read the shape precisely: the model didn't invent a fact from nowhere. It converted absence of evidence into a stated negative, and a "no" about your PTO policy is exactly as much a factual claim as a "yes." If the handbook grants senior staff a higher rate somewhere else, the agent just confidently misinformed an employee while citing a real page.

The pattern generalizes viciously. Not mentioned becomes "not offered." No exception listed becomes "no exceptions exist." Part-timers not named in the paragraph becomes "part-timers aren't eligible." It's the most dangerous failure class in RAG systems because it looks like grounded behavior: the retrieval happened, the citation is real, only the claim is manufactured.

Why "just fix the prompt" underdelivers

A rule with an example teaches the example more than the rule. Ban the tenure claim specifically and the model stops making that claim, then finds a new unstated negative you didn't enumerate: eligibility, probation, caps. You're playing whack-a-mole with an opponent that generates moles.

What does help: state the general rule as a general rule, refuse to let it read as one example, and sketch the category space. The actual prompt text (appended to the handbook rules):

This is a general principle, not limited to one example: a retrieved
chunk simply not mentioning something is NEVER grounds to state a
negative or exclusion as fact. This applies to every kind of unstated
claim — tenure-based changes, eligibility restrictions (e.g. don't
say part-time employees are excluded from a benefit unless a chunk
explicitly excludes them), exceptions, caps, anything. Before adding
any qualifier, aside, or 'note:' to your answer, check it is
explicitly stated in a retrieved chunk — if it isn't, cut it, even
if it seems like a reasonable inference.

Every clause is doing work. "Not limited to one example" exists because models treat examples as boundaries. The named categories sketch the space. And "even if it seems like a reasonable inference" targets the exact spot these fabrications live, because silence-as-negation is reasonable inference. That's why the model keeps doing it. It's just not grounded.

A sibling rule closes the numeric side (the compare lesson at system scale):

Do not state any numeric claim, ratio, or comparison in your final
answer unless it came directly from a tool result earlier in this
conversation — if you haven't called a tool for a number, leave it out.

The principle that organizes everything after this

After all that careful prompt work, is the failure gone? No. Reduced, meaningfully. Gone, no. And that's not a wording defect a better sentence would fix; it's the nature of the instrument.

The principle

What you can check syntactically, fix with code. What requires judging meaning, a prompt can only make less frequent, never eliminate.

Whether a cited page appears in the retrieved set is syntactic: code, with certainty. Whether a similarity score clears a threshold is arithmetic: code. Whether the model burned two searches with nothing to show is a counter: code. Whether a sentence is "reasonable inference" versus "grounded claim" requires judging meaning, so it lives in the prompt, and the prompt's honest job is pushing the failure rate down while code catches the checkable consequences of whatever leaks through.

That's what "layered guardrails" means as an architecture instead of a slogan: the prompt reduces, the code verifies what's verifiable, and neither pretends to do the other's job.

The design test to keep

Takeaway

Prompts are requests, not guarantees. For every reliability rule you write, ask: if the model ignored this sentence, what catches it? If the answer is nothing, you have a request where you need a structure.

You'll watch a model ignore a perfectly clear instruction with the whole system watching on the composition page, where the fix is removing the option rather than sharpening the request.
